## 1. Executive summary

**Decision question.** For an experienced recreational lifter (potentially also a runner), when should a coach increase, maintain, reduce or redistribute working sets during a strength-development block? The primary outcome is improvement in a prioritized lift's standardized maximal strength; secondary considerations are adherence, training time, fatigue, secondary sport commitments and hypertrophy where relevant. The central comparator is a productive existing dose, not inactivity. The intervention of interest is a longitudinal feedback-driven decision policy, not merely a high-volume program.

### Findings and implications

1. **More sets can improve strength, but additional sets are not invariably beneficial.** Enes et al. (2024, men) found larger squat gains with predetermined escalation; Enes et al. (2025, women) found larger leg-press gains in both escalation groups. Fixed-dose trials also show benefits of additional sets at low doses, alongside null or uncertain contrasts at other doses. These findings support keeping an increase available, not requiring one.
2. **Important refinement to Packet 001:** the female Enes trial did not start all arms at 22 weekly sets. Its manuscript specifies starts of 22, 16 and 18 sets. The smaller-escalation arm accumulated fewer prescribed quadriceps sets than the constant arm (252 vs 264), yet had greater leg-press improvement. This is suggestive evidence against the simple explanation that *more cumulative sets alone* explains every observed advantage. It still does not isolate a causal benefit of the escalation pattern independent of starting dose, acclimation, final exposure, realized load or fatigue. The contrast was not designed as a matched-total-dose or equivalence comparison. Verify manuscript against the version of record before acceptance.
3. **Habitual dose matters as a baseline for decisions, not as a validated percentage multiplier.** Barsuhn et al. (2025) found strength improvements in trained men maintaining prior set counts as well as those increasing them. Moreno et al. (2025) found no demonstrated advantage to increasing vs maintaining baseline elbow-flexion volume. Scarpelli et al. (2022) supports individualized baseline-informed prescriptions for a hypertrophy endpoint, not a strength trigger. None establishes a universal relative increase.
4. **Diminishing returns are supported at the population level.** Pelland et al. (2026) found a positive but strongly diminishing association of weekly fractional set count with strength gains. Its curve is not a personal marginal response model or a safety ceiling. Additional sets consume time; perceptual responses do not consistently rise in parallel with set count. Neither an individual maximum recoverable volume nor an inverted-U fatigue curve has been established here.
5. **Feedback-driven adjustment remains an efficacy gap.** The main Enes trials assigned set increments on a calendar; load was adjusted to maintain effort. Barsuhn and Moreno individualized starting doses but did not assign weekly set counts from ongoing response. Helms et al. (2018) actually used RPE stops to determine sets but measured performed volume, not superior chronic strength adaptation vs fixed sets. A 2026 preprint directly tests subjective-feedback-based set autoregulation for hypertrophy; no advantage was detected, and it is not peer-reviewed strength evidence. No identified controlled trial validates RT-P02's proposed feedback-driven weekly strength policy in experienced hybrid athletes.
6. **Recommendation for review:** adopt *maintain a productive dose* as the conservative candidate default, not as a proven universally optimal rule. Consider a bounded increase only when the objective, reliable observed exposure, performance trajectory, other plausible causes of stagnation, tolerability and competing constraints jointly support testing it. Preserve the ability to hold, reduce, redistribute or request information. No new numerical progression thresholds are proposed.

- PICO: adults with prior resistance training, emphasizing experienced populations; working-set volume changes vs maintained or alternative volume programs; objective strength at intervention endpoint; primarily multiweek trials. ### 2.3 Critical appraisal: what the intervention contrasts identify

**Enes male trial (V01–02).** Randomized assigned programs compare same initial set count, different scheduled growth in count, different totals and different final exposures. They provide a credible signal that these specific higher-dose trajectories can improve the tested lift, even at substantial set counts. Since direct squat practice also grows with quadriceps set prescription, the effect is not isolated muscle hypertrophy or a pure mechanical-volume mechanism. The trial did not compare constant 32 or 37 weekly sets with the corresponding escalation arms. Even the constant group progressed loading; *constant set count is not constant stimulus*. The study's test reliability includes a reported minimum detectable difference of 7.86 kg for its squat test, which is a protocol-specific individual measurement-error quantity, not a between-group significance criterion or general progression threshold. Small samples, incomplete follow-up, dietary self-report, and exclusion of additional high-intensity/lower-body exercise constrain generalization. Confirm allocation concealment, analysis population and supplementary details before approval.

**Enes female trial (V03–04).** The key new finding is a low-start trajectory with lower main-phase cumulative set count than CG, and no detected difference in summed VL. This is a useful counterexample to a claim that escalation benefits necessarily reflect *more total sets*. However, it does not prove an independent benefit of escalation at equal cumulative dose: neither set counts nor preparatory doses were matched, and tonnage was not assigned/equated. Tonnage can be affected by strength itself; a non-significant contrast is not dose equivalence. Different early fatigue, practice, baseline strength and other time-varying mediators are plausible, not demonstrated causal mechanisms. Confirm the exact version-of-record Methods, Fig. 4B/C, analysis and reported pairwise intervals. Retain the kg contrast CIs rather than attempting to reconcile manuscript standardized-effect intervals with kg intervals without raw data. No detected difference between 4SG and 2SG is not a proof of equivalence or an optimal lower dose. Training experience also differs across groups and between male/female studies; do not infer sex-specific optimal escalation from separate trials.

**Pelland (V05).** The primary analysis combines outcome-specific set definitions: a direct set corresponds to the assessed exercise or muscle; indirect sets receive a partial weight in the preferred fractional coding. The strength model uses mean fractional weekly volume, not a time-varying within-block progression slope. Eligibility included randomized studies ≥4 weeks, published/preprinted before June 2024, controlled load and proximity to failure within specified tolerances. The full-text reproduction reports that the strength synthesis includes 66 studies and 490 dependent effects; overall sample across outcomes is 67 studies/2,058 individuals, 79.1% male. Distinguish these denominators. The authors report qualitatively consistent analyses restricted to direct within-study comparisons (32 studies, 257 effects, 972 participants); inspect OSF outputs and inclusion mapping rather than treating each analysis as independent evidence. The mean strength-study fractional volume reported in Discussion was 8.14±6.23. That sample region differs materially from the high quadriceps totals in Enes. The mean slope is conditional/marginalized over other model variables and not a constant percentage improvement per added set everywhere on the curve. The fitted reciprocal shape approaches a plateau; it is not an experimentally identified hard cap or a model of fatigue cost. Prediction uncertainty and unexplained variance matter more for individual prescriptions than certainty about the average slope. The authors' Table 4 efficiency tiers use their smallest detectable effect definition; do **not** convert them into product limits, target sets, progression steps or personal minimum effective volume. Fractional counting performed well relative to other coding choices in that dataset; it does not validate a universal 0.5 coefficient for every accessory or athlete. Store direct/indirect categories and version any proposed derived weights separately. Review model priors, model comparison, heterogeneity, study-level bias/quality, high-dose support, training-status interaction and sensitivity to influential studies before finalizing. Male Enes is listed in the review's references; verify actual effect inclusion in OSF. Female Enes and newer trials postdate the search cutoff. Avoid double counting a trial and meta-regression as independent votes.

**Habitual volume (V06–09).** Starting from prior exposure has face validity and reduces interpretation problems from assigning everyone an identical dose. Barsuhn actually tests baseline maintenance vs individualized increases and reports a time effect without significant group × time interaction for maximal strength. Its group main effect and larger control posttest values are vulnerable to baseline imbalance. Attrition (26/55) and broad baseline exposure variance limit precision and generalizability. The larger raw control improvement is not a statistically established superiority claim; nor is the trial evidence of equivalence. Scarpelli tests an initial individualized prescription, not weekly responsiveness; outcome is CSA. Aube tests fixed absolute set targets with baseline-informed randomization, not adaptation. Moreno's direct comparison and control group details need inspection, but its reported outcome is an additional reason not to assume that prior-volume increases are mandatory. Baseline dose may be an effect modifier, a proxy for experience/tolerance, or a consequence of past choices; no validated individual dose-response function follows.

**Diminishing returns and burden (V09–12).** Low-dose improvements in the athlete trial and ambiguous/null contrasts at higher fixed doses fit a diminishing-returns interpretation, but not a universal optimal set count. Longer sessions have a direct practical cost. Derived strain metrics can increase mechanically with count/duration; sRPE and soreness are not independent measures of adaptation capacity. V12 is explicitly a secondary analysis of V01, so it adds outcomes rather than independent participants. It neither validates a fatigue score threshold nor shows that escalation caused harm. Existing Packet 001 E12 (Saw) supports collecting well-being observations; it does not establish an intervention effect of reacting to those observations. Hypertrophy results should not be substituted for strength results.

### 2.5 Causal question: does escalating volume help independent of total dose?

Let `V_t` denote assigned set count in week t, `D = sum(V_t)` its cumulative assigned dose, and `Y` end-block strength. A policy is a mapping from history `H_t` to next prescription, rather than a scalar dose. Enes male randomization identifies the effect of whole trajectories on average, not the separate causal effect of slope holding D fixed. Enes female 2SG is a useful lower-total counterexample but differences in starting and preparatory exposure, realized intensity, final exposure and nonlinear response preclude isolating the slope. Adjusting for realized tonnage after randomization is not automatically valid: it can be both a mediator and affected by time-varying performance.

A future trial should compare feasible scheduled escalating vs constant-volume trajectories with matched planned cumulative *movement-specific* sets, comparable load/effort/frequency, prior-dose stratification, standardized test timing and documented adherence. If different initial or final doses are unavoidable, define the estimand as the whole trajectory contrast rather than a pure slope effect. An additional adaptive-policy arm would estimate the value of using feedback, ideally against both fixed and scheduled alternatives. Even matched set counts cannot equate all physiological exposure. Do not post hoc select only those who completed every set; report intention-to-treat and exposure/attrition alongside any prespecified per-protocol analysis.

### Decision procedure (ordered, with qualitative predicates only)

| Priority / condition | Candidate action | Rationale and evidence status |
|---|---|---|
| A. Hard restriction, acute illness, new or worsening pain, incompatible context or no safe feasible schedule | Block escalation; route to safety or block review; defer if necessary | Existing safety/authority constraint, not an effect estimate. Do not diagnose injury or overtraining. |
| B. History or mapping insufficient to establish actual exposure, performance or adherence | Retain a safe approved prescription or ask for clarification; never increase based on missing records | Engineering safeguard. Missing is unknown, not zero, failure or evidence of tolerance. |
| C. Protected assessment/taper or secondary objective would be compromised | Preserve approved phase intent and exposure bounds; consider redistribution only when feasible and consistent with intent | Packet 001 phase/secondary-goal methodology; no specific taper or concurrent rule validated by RT-02. |
| D. Current dose is productive: comparable strength/performance trajectory consistent with goal and phase, tolerable burden, adequate adherence | Maintain set count as preferred candidate; progress load/reps independently if RT-P01 warrants | V06/V07 and fixed-dose trials support possibility of gains without increasing sets. Preference to hold is a risk/time/opportunity-cost judgment, not proof that hold maximizes 1RM. |
| E. Apparent stall with uncertain adherence, inconsistency, altered movement or effort, or plausible alternative explanation | Investigate; hold or revise execution/scheduling; do not classify as insufficient dose | Inference safeguard. Compare achieved, not merely prescribed exposure. |
| F. Credible stagnation despite relevant completed exposure, sufficiently comparable outcomes, tolerable effort/burden, feasible competing objectives | Consider a bounded volume increase as one testable option; compare with maintaining dose, adjusting specificity/load/reps or redistributing exposure | V01/V03/V05/V11 support plausibility; evidence does not tell us which individual will benefit. Candidate, not automatic action. |
| G. Sustained credible excessive burden, reduced adherence or deteriorating performance with relevant feedback | Consider holding, reducing or redistributing sets; review other explanations and safety constraints; protect essential goal-specific exposures when feasible | V12 supports tracking costs, not a proven fatigue-triggered reduction rule. Avoid claiming a specific cause without evidence. |
| H. Prior volume change already in evaluation | Review its effects and feasibility before escalating again; maintain or revert when warranted by goals, burden or restrictions | Conservative experimentation methodology; cannot prove causality from before/after observations. |

Ordering matters: restrictions and data quality precede progression. When conditions conflict (e.g., improving priority performance but worsening secondary goal or high burden), do not silently select whichever signal favors more sets. Propose tradeoff review with feasible alternatives. A planned set increase may still be appropriate during accumulation even while progress is occurring if there is a separately justified block-level objective; positive feedback alone is not sufficient. Maintaining an inadequate or unsafe prescription is not the default.

**Adherence and performance handling.** Distinguish prescribed slots/sets, confirmed completed sets, partial or explicitly missed work, and unknown exposure. Compare consistent movement variants, equipment, load conventions, technique and effort. Use actual standardized 1RM when available, otherwise comparable load/repetition/effort and clearly labeled estimates. Do not interpret one low estimated 1RM as stagnation or one high estimate as evidence of adaptation. Subjective fatigue, soreness, enjoyment and RPE are separate fields; consider context and reliability, including work stress, sleep, nutrition and running workload when available, without requiring advanced sensor data. No signal-to-action threshold or universal rolling-window length is established.

## 4. Periodization methodology

RT-02 informs volume decisions within the existing longitudinal block model; it does not prescribe a new block duration or phase sequence. See Packet 001 LP-P01 and RT-P01 for phase-aware programming and load/repetition progression, and CT-P01 for secondary objectives. The following is candidate methodology, not a result from a randomized trial of adaptive periodization.

| Block decision | Empirical boundary | Candidate methodology |
|---|---|---|
| Initialize dose | Habitual-volume studies test specific baseline-relative prescriptions with mixed results; no validated multiplier | Use recent representative, comparable completed exposure as the reference; record historical range and recent interruptions. Select a feasible starting prescription consistent with goal, effort, prior tolerance and secondary workload. If uncertain, ask rather than use a universal default. |
| Accumulation | Some scheduled higher-dose trajectories improved tested strength; their timing effect not isolated | Make volume escalation optional. Retain successful work, or test a reviewed increase when the objective warrants it. Preserve sufficient capacity for completion and other training. |
| Intensification | This packet does not establish an optimal volume/intensity tradeoff | Allow stable or reduced sets with increased movement specificity/load when justified by the approved strength objective; do not force an increase in both dimensions. Separate set count from intensity and effort. |
| Assessment/recovery | RT-02 did not evaluate tapering or deload protocols | Respect protected assessment windows and review separate taper/deload evidence (Packet 001 LP-P02). No catch-up sets to meet a cumulative-volume target at the expense of phase intent. |
| Subsequent block | Within-athlete prior response is observational and not automatically causal | Compare achieved exposure, comparable outcomes, adherence and burden with the preceding block; retain effective components and document why another change is warranted. Do not reset to an arbitrary population average. |
| Hybrid coordination | No focal trial validates dose adjustments under concurrent running; Packet 001 concurrent evidence remains contextual | Maintain explicit endurance/non-priority floors and outcomes. Track total schedule burden and running changes separately; if desired strength dose is infeasible under secondary commitments, show tradeoffs rather than silently drop runs. |

### Proposed measurement contract

- Preserve raw working-set counts at movement level and a separate mapping of target-muscle and lift-specific direct/indirect exposure. Record unilateral counting convention, warm-up vs working status, incomplete sets, technique/variant and effort where available. Do not apply Pelland's fractional weights as a hidden hard-coded conversion.
- Keep both weekly prescribed and actual cumulative exposure in the reviewed block. Load × reps × sets, direct sets and fractional set counts are different measures; no conversion between them is universally valid.
- Define habitual baseline from a representative period of comparable recorded training with disclosed coverage; do not treat a holiday, deload, disrupted week or incomplete import as a true low baseline. No fixed baseline window proposed.
- Maintain versioned review state for the last adjustment (what changed, when, objective, competing explanations, outcome to monitor, burden and next review). Distinguish change in the plan from execution choices within its approved bounds. Do not infer a personal dose-response curve from sparse before/after observations.

### Prospective evaluation of benefit (not just software conformance)

A useful baseline is stable volume with standard load/repetition progression and matched review attention. Compare against a fixed planned escalation and candidate RT-P02. Prespecify a primary movement-specific strength endpoint, smallest worthwhile change informed by measurement reliability and athlete goals (not supplied here), secondary outcomes including endurance retention, adherence, elapsed training time, session effort, adverse symptoms and burden, and missing-data handling. Analyze assigned-policy effects, not only successful completers. Record actual doses and nonadherence separately. In personalized experiments, carryover, training-age trends, regression to the mean, adaptation latency and changing fatigue complicate crossover/N-of-1 interpretation; consider multiple blocks, standardized assessment and prospective hypotheses rather than causal claims from one adjustment. If collecting observational policy data, log action alternatives and decision context; time-varying confounding prevents naive regression of outcome on actual set count from being a causal personalized prescription model. An RCT of the adaptive policy is a stronger test of policy value. Sample size, intervals, practical effect margins and follow-up design require separate scientific review.
